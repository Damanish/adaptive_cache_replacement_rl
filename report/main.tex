\begin{abstract}
Cache replacement policies dictate memory hierarchy efficiency in modern computing systems. Classical deterministic heuristics (LRU, LFU, FIFO) operate under rigid assumptions that catastrophically degrade when workload dynamics shift—such as when a Zipf-distributed working set transitions to sequential streaming or cyclic access patterns. This paper formulates adaptive cache replacement as a Reinforcement Learning (RL) problem over macro-decision windows. We design an observation space integrating causal shadow-cache indicators, reuse-distance histograms, and key-space entropy, paired with a variance-reduced relative reward formulation. We implement a zero-flush multi-policy cache simulator with exponentially aged frequencies and evaluate Tabular Q-Learning and Double DQN against static baselines, Follow-The-Leader (FTL), Megiddo \& Modha's Adaptive Replacement Cache (ARC), and Belady's theoretical optimum. Our learned agents outperform all static policies and ARC across multi-phase non-stationary traces, achieving rapid recovery following workload transitions.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction \& Problem Formulation}
Modern processor, OS page, and storage caches rely on eviction algorithms to maximize hit rates and minimize Average Memory Access Time ($\text{AMAT} = h \cdot L_{\text{hit}} + (1-h) \cdot L_{\text{miss}}$). While Least Recently Used (LRU) excels under temporal locality and Least Frequently Used (LFU) thrives under stationary popularity skews, both suffer pathology: LRU is vulnerable to scan pollution and cyclic loops ($L > C$), while classical LFU suffers from cache pollution when formerly hot items become dormant.

Rather than committing to a fixed policy or rigid hand-tuned heuristics, we train an RL agent to continuously inspect access statistics and select the optimal eviction policy ($\text{LRU}, \text{LFU}, \text{FIFO}$, or $\text{MRU}$) for upcoming request windows. 

\subsection{Non-Stationary Workload Regimes}
To rigorously model real-life systems, we construct synthetic multi-phase traces alternating between five canonical access patterns:
\begin{itemize}[noitemsep]
    \item \textbf{Zipf ($\alpha \in [0.9, 1.2]$):} Power-law skew favoring frequency-based caching (LFU).
    \item \textbf{Working Set Drift:} Hot items shift to a disjoint address space every few hundred requests; LRU adapts quickly while un-aged LFU fails.
    \item \textbf{Sequential Scan:} A hot working set interspersed with long unrepeated streams; recency policies suffer cache flushing.
    \item \textbf{Stack Distance (Geometric):} High temporal recency favoring LRU.
    \item \textbf{Looping Workload ($L > C$):} Sequential access over a working set exceeding capacity; LRU/FIFO thrash, while frequency or MRU preserves hits.
\end{itemize}

\section{Reinforcement Learning Formulation}
Per-request RL decisions incur prohibitive inference overhead. We formulate the problem as a \textit{semi-Markov decision process} over decision windows of $W = 100$ requests. At window $t$, the agent observes window-level summary statistics and chooses an eviction policy $a_t \in \mathcal{A}$ governing all cache misses in $[tW, (t+1)W)$.

\subsection{State Space $\mathcal{S}$}
To ensure causal validity, observations $\mathbf{s}_t \in \mathbb{R}^d$ ($d=19$ for 3 actions, $d=23$ for 4 actions) are computed strictly from requests prior to $tW$:
\begin{enumerate}[noitemsep]
    \item \textbf{Shadow-Cache Hit Rates ($2k$ dims):} Instantaneous hit rates and exponential moving averages (EMA, $\beta=0.8$) of lightweight resident shadow caches running each candidate policy in parallel.
    \item \textbf{Stream Distribution Metrics ($6$ dims):}
    \begin{itemize}[noitemsep]
        \item \textit{Unique Ratio}: $U_t / W$, indicating working-set density.
        \item \textit{Sequential Stride Ratio}: Fraction of consecutive accesses $k_i = k_{i-1} + 1$, signaling scans.
        \item \textit{Cold-Start Ratio}: Fraction of keys never seen prior.
        \item \textit{Reuse-Distance Buckets}: Fraction of re-references with reuse gap $\Delta \le C$ and $C < \Delta \le 4C$.
        \item \textit{Popularity Entropy}: Normalized Shannon entropy $H = -\sum p_i \ln p_i / \ln W$.
        \item \textit{Jaccard Overlap}: $|K_t \cap K_{t-1}| / |K_t \cup K_{t-1}|$ between successive windows, signaling phase boundaries.
    \end{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Internal Cache State \& History ($k + 3$ dims):} Agent's own recent hit rate $h_t$, hit rate EMA, fraction of resident blocks with access frequency equal to 1 (dead-block indicator), and one-hot encoding of the prior action $a_{t-1}$.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Action Space $\mathcal{A}$}
In the primary formulation, $\mathcal{A} = \{0: \text{LRU}, 1: \text{LFU}, 2: \text{FIFO}\}$. In our extended model, we introduce $\text{MRU}$ (Most Recently Used, $a=3$), which provides theoretical immunity to cyclic loops ($L > C$) and scan pollution.

\subsection{Reward Function $\mathcal{R}$}
The raw window hit rate $h_t$ is heavily confounded by the underlying phase (e.g., scan phases have a maximum achievable hit rate of 0.20, whereas drift phases yield 0.87). Using unnormalized rewards introduces severe variance into temporal difference targets. We design a \textbf{variance-reduced relative reward}:
\begin{equation}
    r_t = 2 \cdot \left( h_t - h_t^{\text{shadow-LRU}} \right) - \lambda \cdot \mathbf{1}_{\{a_t \neq a_{t-1}\}}
\end{equation}
Because the shadow-LRU hit rate $h_t^{\text{shadow-LRU}}$ depends solely on the exogenous request sequence and is independent of the agent's actions, subtracting it acts as a state-dependent baseline. This preserves the exact optimal policy $\pi^*$ while removing non-stationary variance across phases. The optional switching penalty $\lambda \ge 0$ prevents high-frequency chattering. For system evaluations, we map hit rate to memory latency:
\begin{equation}
    r_{\text{latency}} = -\frac{h_t \cdot L_{\text{hit}} + (1 - h_t) \cdot L_{\text{miss}}}{L_{\text{miss}}}
\end{equation}
where $L_{\text{hit}} = 1$ and $L_{\text{miss}} = 100$ cycles.

\section{Cache Dynamics \& Algorithms}

\subsection{Multi-Policy Cache Simulator}
A naive policy switch that flushes or rebuilds cache structures incurs extreme performance penalties. We implement a unified \texttt{MultiPolicyCache} storing capacity $C=100$. Each cache slot tracks three metadata fields simultaneously: last access timestamp $t_{\text{last}}$, insertion timestamp $t_{\text{ins}}$, and access frequency $f$. When a miss occurs under action $a_t$, the victim is selected via the corresponding metadata vector without modifying cache contents.

\textbf{Exponential Frequency Aging:} Unmodified LFU suffers from permanent residency of obsolete blocks. We apply continuous exponential decay with half-life $H = 10C$:
\begin{equation}
    f_i(t) = f_i(t_{\text{last}}) \cdot 2^{-(t - t_{\text{last}})/H} + 1
\end{equation}
Victims for LFU are chosen by minimizing $f_i(t)$, breaking ties by least-recent access time.

\subsection{Reinforcement Learning Agents}
\textbf{1. Tabular Q-Learning:} To evaluate a lightweight, low-memory embedded agent, we discretize continuous features into a compact state space of $N = |\mathcal{A}| \times 3 \times 4 \times |\mathcal{A}| \times 2 = 216$ states ($576$ for 4 actions) based on the dominant shadow cache, margin of victory, own hit rate, previous action, and Jaccard change flag. Q-values update via standard Bellman updates with learning rate $\alpha = 0.1$ and discount $\gamma = 0.9$.

\textbf{2. Double Deep Q-Network (Double DQN):} To exploit the full continuous observation space, we implement a multi-layer perceptron (two hidden layers of 128 units with ReLU activations). We mitigate Q-value overestimation using Double Q-learning \cite{vanhasselt2016deep}:
\begin{equation}
    y_t = r_t + \gamma \, Q(s_{t+1}, \operatorname{argmax}_{a'} Q(s_{t+1}, a'; \theta); \theta^-)
\end{equation}
with target network parameters $\theta^-$ updated via Polyak averaging ($\tau = 0.01$), experience replay buffer of 50,000 transitions, Adam optimizer ($\text{lr} = 5 \times 10^{-4}$), and smooth $L_1$ Huber loss.

\subsection{Baselines \& Benchmarks}
We benchmark against: (1) \textbf{Fixed-LRU, Fixed-LFU, Fixed-FIFO, Fixed-MRU}; (2) \textbf{Random Policy}; (3) \textbf{Follow-The-Leader (FTL)}: an online greedy heuristic selecting the best shadow policy from the preceding window; (4) \textbf{Phase-Oracle}: a clairvoyant supervisor selecting the best static policy for each phase; (5) \textbf{ARC (Adaptive Replacement Cache)}: Megiddo \& Modha's self-tuning two-list cache \cite{megiddo2003arc}; and (6) \textbf{Belady's MIN}: the clairvoyant offline optimum \cite{belady1966study}.

\subsection{Overall Hit Rate \& Memory Latency}
As summarized in Figure~\ref{fig:overall} and Table~\ref{tab:comparison}, Double DQN achieves the highest overall hit rate among all non-clairvoyant policies. In the standard 3-action setting, DQN attains an average hit rate of 57.50\% ($\text{AMAT} = 43.08$ cycles), outperforming Fixed-LRU (54.71\%, AMAT = 45.84 cycles), Follow-The-Leader (55.73\%), and the Adaptive Replacement Cache (ARC, 57.11\%). In the extended 4-action setting (+MRU), DQN scales to 63.58\% hit rate ($\text{AMAT} = 37.06$ cycles), delivering an 19.2\% latency reduction over static LRU and closely approaching Belady's theoretical limit (68.63\%).

\subsection{Phase Recovery \& Dynamic Policy Adaptation}
Figure~\ref{fig:timeline} details policy adaptation across a representative scenario switching from Zipf $\to$ Scan $\to$ Drift $\to$ Zipf. During the Zipf phase, DQN shifts to LFU to exploit frequency skew. When a sudden cold scan begins, recency policies collapse, but DQN swiftly recognizes the plummet in Jaccard similarity and shadow-cache indicators, shifting away from polluted recency stacks. Upon transitioning to working set drift, DQN immediately triggers LRU, capturing the shifting hot set while static LFU drops to near-zero hit rates.

\subsection{Generalization Across Cache Capacities}
To evaluate scalability, agents trained strictly on $C=100$ were tested without retraining on $C \in \{50, 200\}$. Hit rates scaled consistently (43.2\% at $C=50$, 62.4\% at $C=200$), maintaining superiority over static baselines and confirming that our feature normalization enables zero-shot capacity transfer.

\section{Conclusion}
This work demonstrates that adaptive cache replacement under changing workloads can be effectively mastered via reinforcement learning. By framing policy selection over macro-windows, leveraging strictly causal shadow-cache features, and employing variance-reduced relative rewards, our Double DQN agent achieves superior hit rates, rapid recovery, and substantial latency reductions over traditional and adaptive caching baselines.
